Marketing is usually judged by how much attention it produces: posts published, messages received,
enquiries coming in. That's the visible half. The half that decides whether any of it matters
happens afterward, and it's much quieter — what actually happens to an enquiry in the hours and
days after it arrives.

In my experience, that second half is where most marketing quietly breaks. Not because the content
was weak or the audience was wrong, but because nothing dependable connected the attention to a
real conversation.

## 01 — The Half Nobody Watches Closely

A lot of marketing effort is aimed at generating leads. Far less is aimed at what a lead
experiences once it exists. The pattern is familiar:

- An enquiry arrives in a message thread, a comment, or a direct message, and lives only there.
- Whoever sees it first replies when they get to it — or doesn't.
- There's no single record of who asked, when, and what was said back.
- When a lead does get passed to Sales, it arrives as a name, without the context needed to act on
  it.
- Nobody can answer a simple question: of the people who reached out this week, what happened to
  each of them?

None of this looks like failure on any given day. Each dropped enquiry is small and invisible. The
cost only shows up in aggregate, as leads that "just didn't convert" — when some of them were never
really followed up at all.

## 02 — Attention Is Not a System

Content, on its own, is activity. A content calendar can be full and disciplined and still not be
connected to anything: posts go out, some attention comes back, and the link between the two is
whatever happens to be remembered.

A system is what makes the connection reliable rather than accidental. It answers, in advance:
where does an enquiry get recorded, who is responsible for the first reply, how quickly, and what
happens next. Without those answers written down, the outcome depends on who is busy and who
remembers — which is the same failure that appears in operations everywhere else, just wearing a
marketing label.

## 03 — Five Steps Between Attention and a Conversation

The way I think about it is a short chain, where every link has to exist for the whole thing to
hold: **Attract → Capture → Respond → Hand off → Review.**

**Attract** is the part everyone already thinks about — the content and the work that puts you in
front of the right people.

**Capture** is recording every enquiry in one place the moment it arrives, so it exists somewhere
other than a chat thread.

**Respond** is a defined, timely first reply — not a good intention, but an expectation with a
clock on it.

**Hand off** is passing a qualified lead to whoever needs to act on it, with enough context that
they don't have to start the conversation from scratch.

**Review** is a regular look at what came in and what happened to it, so the process improves
instead of repeating.

This is a practical working model, not a formal methodology. It's simply what the real steps look
like when they're made explicit — and many marketing setups are strong at the first step and
improvised at the rest.

## 04 — What Owning Both Halves Taught Me

At Perla Engineering, I owned content and lead handling at the same time, and that turned out to be
the useful constraint. The challenge was doing two different jobs — producing content, and
capturing and qualifying the leads that content generated — without either one slipping whenever
the other got busy.

The approach was to stop treating it as one blurry role and split it into two explicitly owned
tracks, each with its own daily and weekly rhythm, then connect them with one shared system:
content that generates leads, and a process that makes sure every lead gets a timely reply and a
clean handoff. The point of the split wasn't bureaucracy. It was that a job with no defined
rhythm gets done in whatever time is left over, which is usually none.

## 05 — Rules Beat Good Intentions

The part of that system I'd defend most strongly is that it ran on rules someone could check,
rather than on people trying their best. A new lead was logged within an hour and replied to
within two, using a template. Qualified leads went to Sales the same day, with the full context.
Anything left unanswered after 24 hours got followed up.

Every one of those is checkable: it either happened inside the window or it didn't. That's the
difference between a standard and an intention. Templates mattered for a related reason — they
removed the small decision of what to say, which is exactly the decision that gets postponed when
things are busy.

The content side had a rule of its own: nothing gets posted that isn't real project work. It's a
small rule, but it's a positioning decision written as a constraint. It means the content can
never drift into filler just to keep the calendar full, and that whatever a lead sees before
reaching out is something the business can actually stand behind.

## 06 — Statuses Are a Language

The lead tracker used four statuses: new, contacted, meeting, lost. That's a deliberately small
vocabulary, and its value is that anyone can read the state of the pipeline at a glance without
asking anyone.

"Lost" is worth a note, because it's the one most trackers avoid. A lead marked lost is
information — it says the conversation happened and didn't lead anywhere. A lead that silently
disappears is not information; it's a gap. Giving unsuccessful outcomes a name is what makes them
countable, and countable is the first step to learning from them.

A short weekly summary — total leads, where they came from, and their status — closed the loop. It
went to management, which meant the process was visible to the people who depended on it, not just
to the person running it.

## 07 — What I Can and Can't Claim

It would be easy to end this with a number, so I want to be direct about why I won't attach one to
this system.

My CV records a 30% increase in lead generation during my time in this role. But I can't say that
figure came specifically from this system rather than from marketing activity more broadly, and I
don't want to imply a cause I can't show. What I can say with confidence is narrower and, I think,
more useful: after the system was in place, the question "what happened to that lead?" always had
an answer. Before it, there was no consistent way to give one.

## 08 — A Practical Test

If you want to know whether marketing attention is turning into real conversations or leaking out
along the way, five questions are usually enough:

1. Can you say how many enquiries arrived this week, and where each came from?
2. Does every enquiry have a status — not just a reply, but a recorded state?
3. Is there a named owner for the first response, and a time by which it should happen?
4. When a lead is handed to someone else, do they receive context, or only a name?
5. Does anyone see a regular summary of what came in and what happened to it?

If several of those are uncomfortable to answer, the constraint probably isn't the content. It's
what happens after it.

## Where this leaves the work

Attention is the visible part of marketing, and it's the part that's easy to celebrate. But it only
becomes a business result when something dependable happens to it after it arrives. Build that
part as carefully as the content — structure, a timely response, a clean handoff, and a regular
look at what happened — and the same effort in content starts to compound instead of leak.
